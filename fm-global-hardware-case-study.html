02 — Research Framing

The Challenge

Field Engineers at FM Global rely on mobile devices to conduct site visits, document risk assessments, and prepare detailed reports. However, existing hardware was creating friction—poor battery life, inadequate performance, and mismatched capabilities were disrupting workflows and undermining the quality of client interactions.

Before investing in new hardware, the organization needed a data-informed understanding of what field engineers actually need to succeed.

Problem Statement
Field engineers need reliable, fit-for-purpose devices that support their work across three distinct phases: pre-visit planning, on-site assessment, and post-visit reporting. The current hardware fails to meet these needs, creating workarounds, inefficiencies, and risks to both engineer safety and client relationships.

Research Goals — What We Set Out to Understand:

01

Battery Requirements

Identify the minimum number of battery hours field engineers expect their device to last between charges during a full site visit.

02

Critical Capabilities

Determine which applications and features are most valuable during field visits—and which are dealbreakers if unavailable.

03

Input Method Preferences

Understand which input methods (stylus, keyboard, touch, voice) field engineers prefer for different tasks and contexts.

04

Device Configuration

Assess whether field engineers prefer one versatile device or two specialized devices (one for planning/reporting, one for site visits).

05

Audience Segmentation

Identify variations across location, seniority, urban/rural settings, and specialty areas (e.g., Chemical, Flood, Wind) that might require different device configurations.

03 — Methodology

Survey Design & Execution

We designed a Qualtrics survey that ran from January 31 to February 14, inviting approximately 1,100 field engineers via email from leadership. The survey was structured to capture both quantitative patterns and qualitative nuance across the field engineer experience.

Survey Structure — Three Question Types
The survey combined demographic profiling, behavioral estimation, and preference ranking to build a complete picture of field engineer needs and current pain points.
01

Demographic & Context Questions

What we asked: Office location, role, specialty (e.g., Flood, Chemical, Wind), years of experience, urban/rural/suburban visit breakdown.

Why it matters: Understanding audience segmentation allows us to identify whether different groups (senior vs. junior, chemical vs. earthquake specialists, rural vs. urban) have distinct device needs.

02

Behavioral Estimation Questions

What we asked: Estimated battery hours needed per visit, percentage of time using each input method (touch, stylus, keyboard, voice) across three phases—pre-visit planning, during site visit, and report writing.

Why it matters: Behavior varies across workflow phases. A device optimized for report writing (keyboard-heavy) might not work well on-site (stylus + touch). Understanding these patterns informs whether one device can handle all phases or if specialization is needed.

03

Preference & Priority Ranking

What we asked: Rank 10 capabilities from most to least valuable during a site visit (e.g., taking freehand notes, marking up sketches, taking photos, connecting to internet). Choose between one versatile device or two specialized devices.

Why it matters: Rankings reveal what's non-negotiable versus nice-to-have. If "taking notes freehand" ranks #1 but current stylus battery dies mid-visit, that's a critical failure mode. Device configuration preferences (1 vs. 2 devices) help assess whether engineers value convenience or specialization.

Survey Platform

Qualtrics survey with email invitations sent from Scott Anderson (GMFE, OCE, OCM) and local GMFEs. Participation was voluntary and open to all invitees.

Open-Ended Responses

We included open-ended questions throughout to capture nuance: "Anything else you'd like to add about input methods?" and "Describe issues using your device outside your home country."

06 — Device Configuration

Identified Tradeoffs

Field engineers face inherent tradeoffs when choosing device configurations. No single device excels at everything—portability competes with screen size, battery capacity competes with weight, and versatility competes with specialized performance.

One Device vs. Two Devices
No clear consensus emerged—and that's revealing. The question itself exposes a fundamental tension: one device that "does everything adequately" means accepting performance tradeoffs, while two specialized devices means managing more equipment, more charging, and more complexity.
01

Stylus Battery Reliability

Battery-powered stylus (requires AAAA battery replacement) vs Rechargeable stylus (shorter runtime, dies mid-visit)

"I use the stylus from my 'old' tablet that has a AAAA replaceable battery, with the tether. Don't like that the new stylus has to be charged all the time and has no hole for tether."

02

Screen Size vs. Portability

Larger screen with detached keyboard (better for report writing, awkward to carry) vs On-screen keyboard only (more portable, limited screen real estate)

"Onscreen keyboard does not really work due to limited screen real estate." vs "It's difficult to type while on the move and inefficient to pull out and re-attach a keyboard."

03

Performance vs. Convenience

Two devices (laptop for reports, lightweight tablet for site visits) vs One versatile device (no duplication, but compromises performance)

"Having a powerful laptop that you keep at home/hotel for prep and reporting, complemented by a tablet with digitizer for site visits is another good idea."

For the two devices question, I would only prefer this method if there were no drawbacks. If there were drawbacks—low battery life, other issues—then I would prefer one device. A pertinent detail would be easy transfer capabilities for information between the two devices. Otherwise, this would greatly extend the time required and defeat the point.

Critical Insight
One device for everything is a compromise. The tablet lacks performance for report writing and is too bulky on site. To succeed, a two-device strategy must ensure the benefits of fit-for-purpose tools outweigh the complexity of managing more equipment. This requires careful coordination across battery performance, ease of use, and seamless data transfer.

08 — Field Observations

Visit Trailings — Preliminary Findings

01

Tablet is Awkward to Carry

Opportunity: Improve comfort, reduce safety and damage risks—make device easier to secure on body. Carrying the bulky tablet makes climbing ladders less safe. Keyboard disconnected and fell ~20 feet.

02

Bluetooth Mouse Requires Extra Adapter

Opportunity: Limit need for adapters—ensure accessories are compatible with device ports. "If I want to use a mouse, it should just work."

03

Stylus Battery Dies Quickly

Opportunity: Improve stylus battery to ensure this valued tool is always available. "This is a new tablet. I ration my stylus use because it dies quicker than my tablet."

04

Handwriting Challenges

Opportunity: Explore note-taking methods that don't rely on handwriting. "I don't use the stylus because my handwriting is too hard to read. I type with the keyboard."

05

Bright Light / Sunny Conditions

Opportunity: Improve readability—Polaris dark mode. Tablet is difficult to use in bright light.
