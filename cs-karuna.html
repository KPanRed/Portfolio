How might we use design thinking to reduce the psychological stress of long-stay patients in acute care hospitals?

01 — Problem Statement

The Hidden Crisis in Critical Care

Hospitalization is a stressful event that disturbs the physical and psychological well-being of patients — which can in turn affect physical recovery and medical compliance. Being attached to machines, tubes, and masks in a busy, noisy ward, often without access to daylight or clocks, leads to sensory disruption and sleep deprivation.

Patients are often isolated, unable to communicate but aware of other people's suffering or death. Mental health problems are common but frequently go unrecognized in acute care settings, compromising the quality of treatment and outcomes.

45–80%
of critical care patients experience acute stress in the form of panic, fear, depressed mood, anger, hallucinations, or delusions.
— Lieve M Roets-Merken et al., BMJ Open, 2016
Overwhelming evidence indicates that mental health problems are common but often go unrecognized in acute care settings — and that appropriate treatment can alleviate these impediments to well-being.

03 — Primary Research

Voices from the Ward

Interviews were conducted with 3 acute care doctors (14–21 years experience) and 5 long-stay patients (ages 22–44, stays ranging 20–30 days). Their firsthand accounts revealed a consistent gap between physical treatment quality and emotional care.

Nathaniel Butler — Patient
Age 29 · Chef · 20-day stay
"They attended my physical needs with skilled efficiency, but never acknowledged my emotional needs. The treatment procedures were repetitive, boring and annoying."
Vineeth Chada — Patient
Age 34 · Software Engineer · 1-month stay
"I felt like a piece of meat carted here and there. I can feel safe around doctors but they can never give me the feeling of me being with my family."
Bhavyaa Kumar — Patient
Age 25 · Student · 25-day stay
"All he did was talking about my disease and how I am going to suffer from it. The treatment is always one-sided — my concerns should also be taken into consideration."
Richard — Patient
Age 22 · Mechanic · 1-month stay
"Doctors often make me nervous. For me, any kind of non-medical inquiry would make me feel comfortable. They made sure I was taking medication but never asked how I was feeling about them."
"Healthcare's focus on physical disease and bio-medicine is unbalanced. We need to pay much more attention to emotional, psychological and spiritual wellbeing and the huge importance of healing relationships."
Robin Youngson — MD, 21 years experience
"Most of the times, patients don't share their emotional issues. Patients don't feel that we can also cure mental illness. There is a lack of right personnel with the right processes and skills."
Amit Thateshetty — MD, Memorial Health, 14 years experience

06 — Opportunities for Design

From Insight to Action

Five key insights from primary research mapped directly to design opportunities — each targeting the structural gap between physical excellence and emotional deficit in acute care.

Insight 01
Doctors attended physical needs with skilled efficiency, but never acknowledged emotional needs.
Opportunity →
Create a strategy to help doctors treat patients medically and mentally at the same time.
Insight 02
The doctor should always provide information the patient wants and promptly respond to their reactions.
Opportunity →
Develop a separate curriculum based on human interaction for doctors during their residency years.
Insight 03
Doctors often talk only about disease and how patients will suffer from it.
Opportunity →
A background check of patients' medical and mental history so doctors can develop a customized treatment approach.
Insight 04
There is a striking lack of standardization and implementation of effective mental care within acute care hospitals.
Opportunity →
Implement mental health treatment with other treatments as a mandatory setting at any healthcare environment.
Insight 05
Families are left out of most of the current system of care — caregivers receive very little information.
Opportunity →
Develop tools to communicate at an empathetic level — sharing information, hope, and compassionate care with families.
07 — Concept Exploration

Three Directions, One Winner

Three initial concepts were evaluated using PMI (Plus, Minus, Interesting) analysis. Each addressed a different entry point in the doctor training lifecycle.

+8
Workshops at Hospitals
A series of workshops designed to sensitize acute care providers to the importance of patient context and train listening skills. Facilitates communication but limited to current practitioners.
+7
Educational Program
Emotional intelligence integrated with biomedical sciences for upcoming doctors during preclinical and clinical years. High investment required; some overlap with existing electives.
−2
Multi-Care Units
Embedding palliative care across acute care sectors. Scored negatively due to time constraints, add-on burden, and palliative care's rapid independent growth as a specialty.
"Empathy can't be taught by books or in labs. Workshops here seem like the right place because they teach everyone at one platform."
Workshop participant — Medical Professional
08 — Design Criteria

What the Solution Must Do

Must Have
Equal importance to physical care and mental care at any stage of illness. A dose of empathy with the medication. Doctor training in communicational and emotional skills.
Should Have
Trust for doctors among patients and their families to avoid fear. Decision making from a patient's perspective.
Nice to Have
Entertainment activities for patients to reduce stress from treatment and the fear of suffering.
09 — The Solution

Karuna

Karuna — meaning "compassion" in Sanskrit — is a strategy which reduces the psychological stress of long-stay patients in acute care hospitals. It explains the importance of communication skills to doctors from a patient's perspective, and teaches how they feel psychologically during their hospital stay.

Doctors learn how to build interaction skills through a series of workshops conducted by facilitators trained in communication skills. This helps doctors build relationships that invoke empathy and compassion among patients.

10 — Workshop Methodology

How Karuna Works

Designed for residency students and care providers in active acute care practice. The workshop consists of brief presentations and interactive exercises to build awareness, knowledge, skills, and confidence in communication.

Module 01
Patient Perspective
Explains doctors how to engage with the patient and empathize with their concerns. Uses real patient cases from primary research to ground the learning.
Module 02
Active Listening
Educates to use clear, concise language and check for understanding with patients. Allows doctors to work with a colleague to examine a problem and provide mutual feedback.
Module 03
Simulated Interaction
One-on-one patient interactions with a senior doctor. Applies targeted communication skills and problem-solving strategies to planned interactions with patients and families.
Module 04
Family Communication
Provides doctors the value of seeing healthcare situations in context — through patients' eyes — and opportunity to analyze the quality and impact of a doctor-patient interaction.
Module 05
Emotional Intelligence
Identifies specific skills to help doctors explore and appreciate patients' attitudes, values, and feelings — building relationships that invoke genuine emotional experiences.
Framework
Certified Facilitation
Conducted by certified facilitators trained in emotional and communicational skills. A research team constantly develops material from live acute care settings.
11 — Implementation Roadmap

Three Phases to Scale

Phase 01
6 Months — Research & Refinement
Extensive research with patients, residency trainers, and senior doctors at acute care hospitals. Results analyzed to refine the prototype for optimum applicability. Booklet and website refined by healthcare professionals.
Phase 02
6 Months — Diffusion
Workshop introduced to various acute care units to facilitate the innovation process. After six months of diffusion, doctors from multiple hospitals adopt the methodology. Value reflected in patient interaction efficiency.
Phase 03
3 Months — Public Launch
Strategy shared publicly as a framework for better patient experience. Train-the-trainer faculty development courses launched, enabling faculty to conduct workshops at their home organizations.
12 — Conclusion

Compassion as a Design Problem

Through primary research and ethnographic interviews with acute care providers and patients, this project surfaced a structural truth: the gap between physical excellence and emotional care in acute settings is not a character flaw in doctors — it is a design failure in how they are trained and deployed.

Karuna represents the opportunity space between acute care (excellent physical treatment) and palliative care (emotional and spiritual well-being). It is not a separate system — it is a layer of human skill that can be embedded into every interaction, at every stage of illness, without requiring separate infrastructure or budget.

Brand Refinement
Continued development of Karuna's identity before consumer-facing touch points are used to promote the workshop.
Financial Analysis
