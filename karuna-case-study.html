05
Design Criteria
The filters that guided concept selection

Not every good idea is the right idea.
Design criteria decide.

Criteria were derived directly from research findings before any concept generation — to separate ideas that sounded good from ones that were structurally viable.

Must Have — Non-negotiable
Equal importance to physical and mental care at any stage of illness
Empathy integrated alongside clinical treatment — not as a separate pathway
Doctor training in communicational and emotional skills
Should Have — Strong Preference
Trust-building between doctors and patients to reduce institutional fear
Decision-making that incorporates the patient's perspective
Structured family involvement in the care conversation
Nice to Have — Aspirational
Activities that reduce stress from the treatment environment itself
Improved environmental design of the acute care unit
Digital tools supporting ongoing patient-family communication
Why Must Haves emerged from the data
The research showed empathic interaction isn't secondary to physical care — it's parallel to it. Any solution treating emotional support as optional fails structurally.
Why trust is a design requirement, not a soft preference
Dr. Thatishetty confirmed it directly: "Patients don't share their emotional issues." When emotional trust is absent, clinical information quality drops. Trust is not a wellbeing metric — it's a diagnostic infrastructure metric.
Why aspirational criteria were separated out
Separating aspirational criteria prevented scope creep and anchored evaluation to what was achievable within real constraints.
Research Thinking
Design criteria are a decision filter. The residency workshop model satisfied all Must Haves and most Should Haves. The other directions each failed at least one Must Have.

INSIGHT 01

Emotional suffering is systematically invisible — and both sides have accepted this.

Patients stopped disclosing because experience taught them nothing would change. Doctors stopped probing because the system never trained them to. A mutual, unspoken agreement formed — emotional needs go permanently unaddressed.

"Most of the time, patients don't share their emotional issues, which cannot be easily seen on scans or tests." — Dr. Amit Thatishetty
Empathy mapping: patients actively suppressed distress to avoid burdening staff they perceived as overloaded.
The longer the stay, the more pronounced this learned suppression became.
Design Opportunity
Create structured moments that make it safe — and expected — for patients to share emotional states.

INSIGHT 02

Clinical skill does not transfer to emotional skill — and no one ever taught the difference.

Every physician was clinically competent. None had received formal training in emotional communication or active listening. This is a curriculum gap, not a character failure. Medical education trains doctors to treat disease — not to be present with suffering.

"It's out of my field. I am not trained in treating such issues." — Robin Youngson, MD (21 years experience)
All three physicians confirmed: emotional intelligence is assumed, not taught.
"Empathy can't be taught by books or in labs." — Workshop Participant, Physician (prototype testing)
Design Opportunity
Experiential emotional intelligence training at residency — reaching upcoming and practicing doctors simultaneously.
Training gap — formal emotional education received
0 of 3

physicians interviewed reported any formal training in emotional communication, active listening, or psychological support during their medical education.

The paradox: Doctors who care deeply about patients are operating without the tools to translate that care into what patients need. This is a design problem, not a character problem.

INSIGHT 03

Technology is creating distance — even when it's meant to enable connection.

Clinical technology is essential — but it shifted the relational register of patient interactions. Looking at a screen rather than a patient changes the nature of the conversation, even when the information exchanged is identical.

"Technology is almost interwoven into everything I do. I feel that sometimes it is acting as a barrier to get the right kind of human interaction." — Prasannaa, Hospitalist
During bedside interactions, screen-facing posture was the default. Eye contact averaged under 20% of interaction time.
Design Opportunity
Train clinicians in eye-contact-first patterns. Explore EHR designs that promote human-facing posture.

INSIGHT 04

Families are the patients' emotional backbone — excluded by the system that needs them most.

Family was every patient's primary source of emotional stability. Yet the system restricts visiting hours, withholds information, and excludes families from care conversations. Its most powerful emotional resource is structurally unavailable.

"Families are left out of most part of current system of care. Patients and their family members and caregivers are given very less information about what is happening to them or their loved one." — Prasannaa
"I can feel safe around doctors but they can never give me the feeling of me being with my family." — Vineeth Chada
The less information families receive, the more anxious they become — adding to the patient's own stress. A self-reinforcing exclusion cycle.
Design Opportunity
Communication protocols that involve families as care partners — not restricted visitors.

INSIGHT 05

Emotional support is a referral destination — not a feature of everyday care.

When distress is severe enough, the system refers to palliative care or psychiatry. But most patient distress never crosses that threshold — it sits in the ordinary daily experience of care, entirely unaddressed.

"We don't have any psychiatric doctor at our unit. If a patient has a severe mental illness, then we move him to a different ward." — Dr. Amit Thatishetty
Palliative care and hospice address emotional illness — but only as referral-only systems. Acute care sits adjacent to that capacity without accessing it.
45–80% of patients experience acute stress. The referral threshold is far higher. Most suffer without any system response.
Design Opportunity
Integrate baseline emotional support into acute care — as a trained feature of every physician-patient interaction, not a referral option.

For long-stay patients who stay in acute care hospitals — Karuna improves their psychological wellbeing by conducting workshops at residency hospitals that teach both upcoming and practicing doctors to build interaction skills with their patients. By doing this, doctors build relationships that invoke emotional experiences — unlike the present acute care environment.

Researcher reflection

What surprised me most

▾

I expected a technology problem. The actual issue was simpler and harder: medical education treats emotional intelligence as a personal attribute, not a clinical competency. Doctors aren't failing out of indifference — they were never taught what to do.

What the research changed in my thinking

▾

I began expecting a technology opportunity. I ended recommending a workshop. Following the data led me away from the solution I assumed — which is exactly when research is doing its job.

A note on scope

▾

Research was conducted at two hospital systems. Directionally strong but not statistically representative. Karuna is validated as a promising direction — not a proven one. That distinction matters, and it shapes what comes next.

What I would do next

▾

Expand to three additional hospital systems to test transferability across geography and patient demographics. Build outcome metrics that go beyond doctor self-report — patient psychological state at discharge, re-admission rates, and long-term wellbeing data. Develop a family-facing parallel component addressing the information exclusion gap that every patient in this study raised.
