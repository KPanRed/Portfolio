A fintech client's service team was frustrated. Satisfaction surveys revealed a mean composite score of 4/7 — and open comment analysis pointed directly at the Salesforce case page UI. We went in to find out why.

01 — Background

The Problem

An industry-leading fintech client flagged high levels of frustration among service associates using their Salesforce Case Page UI. Open comment analysis surfaced recurring pain points, and the numbers reflected it — a mean composite score of just 4 out of 7 on the Salesforce Service Cloud satisfaction survey.

The research needed to identify not just what was broken, but whether issues were rooted in usability, learnability, or user error — each requiring a fundamentally different type of solution.

The existing Salesforce Case Page — eye icons indicate areas associates were actively looking at. Crossed-out eyes show entire panels that went unnoticed during sessions.

02 — Research Objectives

What We Set Out to Learn

01
Understand Key User Task Issues
Identify where associates were struggling and what information was being missed, skipped, or misused during live calls.
02
Analyze Usability Depth
Distinguish between learnability gaps, recurring usability failures, and error-prone interactions — each pointing toward a different type of fix.
03
Understand UI Visual Problems
Surface why the current visual design was creating friction and where improved hierarchy or layout could remove cognitive load.
04
Uncover Customization Needs & Search Patterns
Learn what associates were actually searching for on the page, and how they were working around the system to meet their own needs.

03 — Research Methodology

How We Researched

What
User Interviews + Usability Testing
Moderated sessions combining open-ended interview questions with observed task performance on the live Salesforce environment.
How
Remote, Moderated
Sessions conducted remotely with screen-sharing to observe real interactions and capture think-aloud commentary.
Who
12 Service Associates
4 with under 2 years of experience. 8 with over 2 years. Mix of full-time and part-time across call, written, and chat channels.
Research scope diagram — comparing the Written/Call case page layout (left) against the Open Chat interface (right). Color-coded panels map to shared and unique UI components across case types.

05 — Key Findings

Six Problem Areas

Each finding is presented as a problem → evidence → recommendation sequence — the same structure used to communicate results to the product team.

01
Visual Blindness
Clutter Leads to Blindness
All 12 out of 12 associates were blind to features outside their established routine. Too many tiers of tabs created visual overload. Without a clear hierarchy, associates scrolled constantly — and the C1 connector blocked content when expanded.
"It has so much information here. It's almost information overload… there's probably a lot of stuff here that may be helpful. It's just there's so much here it's hard to use all of it."
— Clint, Service Associate

"I only look at what I want to look at, which is usually 4 places. I never look here… I never go over here." — Terri

"I'm kind of used to this format now… I just have it memorized." — Mac

Redesign information architecture with a clear visual hierarchy
Design with minimal tabs; consolidate duplicates
C1AS Connect panel blocking the case view when expanded
02
UI Clutter
Contact Information Scattered Across 3 Places
Branch area, location, and holdings links were duplicated. Essential contact details required extra clicks or scrolling. 0 out of 12 associates had ever clicked the Branch Details tab.
Red annotations highlight duplicated fields — Branch, Region, Location, Holdings, and Launch Directory all appear in multiple places on the same page.
0/12
Zero associates had ever clicked the Branch Details tab. Its content was either duplicated elsewhere or irrelevant to their workflows.
Merge duplicate fields — remove branch area code, region code, redundant location
Keep Contact Details + City & State, surfaced above the fold
03
Tab Overload
10 of 12 Associates Never Used the Secondary Tabs
10 out of 12 associates were blind to and never clicked the tabs next to Details. Associates occasionally closed the main tab by accident. Content inside secondary tabs was perceived as low-value — and associates couldn't email branches regardless.
Crossed-out eye icons show tabs that went completely unnoticed across sessions. The action bar below generated new tabs that confused associates.
Remove or reduce secondary tabs; promote important content to the main screen
Re-examine actions that generate new tabs mid-workflow
04
Account Number Visibility
11 of 12 Associates Couldn't Find the Account Number
Associates preferred sharing cases by account number — not case number. Yet 11 out of 12 were blind to the account number shown at the top of the page. Written case associates scrolled below the fold searching for it.
Only 1 of 12 found the account number at the top. 11 of 12 scrolled past it — "Where is the stinking account #.. things get lost down here."
11/12
Missed the account number at the top of the page. Only 1 associate navigated there directly.
Make account number visually prominent above the fold
Explore sticky header behaviour once layout changes are implemented
05
Case Box
Case List Lacked the Details Associates Actually Needed
Associates were time-pressured and needed to scan for relevance fast. The details shown weren't useful for filtering. The case list had real potential to move from perceived clutter to genuine value.
Current Client Cases list — missing case owner, subject, case type, and comment indicators needed for fast triage.

What associates needed to see:

Case owner
Date & time in chronological order
Subject line
Department & case type
Comment indicator
Account number-based search as primary filter
06
Knowledge Articles
12 of 12 Found Suggested Articles Distracting
All 12 associates said knowledge article suggestions took up space and were distracting. 100% preferred searching independently. Several didn't know how to search for knowledge articles at all.
100%
Preferred self-directed search over system-suggested articles. All used the top search bar.
Remove or collapse auto-suggested article panel to free up screen real estate
Provide training on knowledge article search
Einstein Recommendations panel occupying significant space — associates found suggestions distracting and irrelevant.

07 — Training vs. Design

Where Training Is Needed

A key research output was separating problems that required UI improvements from those that required better onboarding and training.

Knowledge Search
Many associates didn't know how to search for knowledge articles — a training gap, not a design flaw.
Comment Box
Simple interactions like expanding the comment box were missed during onboarding.
Dashboard Reports
Reporting capabilities went unused — awareness and training could unlock significant value.
Case List Filters
Filters were underutilized — associates defaulted to manual scanning instead.
Parent–Child Cases
Associates were unaware of or confused by parent–child case relationships.
Case Transfers
Transferring cases was a recurring pain point with no clear guidance in onboarding.

08 — Impact

Research Outcomes

The study gave the product and design teams a clear, prioritized picture of where the interface was failing — separating design problems from training problems. Six actionable problem areas were defined, each with specific redesign recommendations ready to inform the next phase of the Salesforce customization.
